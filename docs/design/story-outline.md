# Detailed Story Outline

Status: **Approved**  
Milestone: **0 — Vision lock**

## Story constraints

- The main story lasts 2–4 hours across several compressed spring weeks.
- The protagonist is a capable young beaver building their first independent
  home.
- Their understanding of home grows from “mine” to “our watershed.”
- Players should learn through cause and effect that beavers are ecosystem
  engineers.
- Ecological relationships remain broadly authentic while time, distance,
  construction effort, and animal behavior may be compressed for fun.
- The story moves upstream through Willowbend, Aspen Meadow, and Moraine Basin.
- An older wildfire in Moraine Basin remains visible and is discussed by the
  residents, but fire itself is not framed as the antagonist. The mixed burn
  includes surviving habitat and active regrowth.
- A warm spring storm accelerates snowmelt for the climax.
- The story has one ending with small acknowledgements of player choices.

## Opening — The quiet creek

The protagonist leaves their family territory to build their first independent
home, carrying a letter from Grandma. Grandma was born at Willowbend and has
often described the lively creek, deep pools, and abundant willow she remembers
from her youth. Her letter encourages the protagonist to seek it out and trust
what they learned as an apprentice.

The protagonist arrives expecting the place that captured their imagination,
but finds a diminished creek and no pond deep enough for a lodge. Choosing to
stay is their own decision rather than an inherited mission.

Grandma's family left Willowbend through ordinary dispersal rather than a
single tragedy. With no resident beaver colony maintaining the old dam complex,
it gradually weakened. Years later, a wildfire in Moraine Basin destabilized
the headwaters, and heavy runoff breached what remained. The wetlands drained,
habitats shrank, and some animals were displaced downstream. This linked
history is intentionally neat enough to establish the short game's conflict
quickly.

The protagonist meets a frog surviving beside Willowbend's last shallow pool.
The frog has watched the creek shrink and is skeptical that a newly arrived
beaver—or one repaired dam—will help. The protagonist first approaches the dam
as a practical way to create enough water for their own lodge; the frog pushes
them to observe how the work affects habitat beyond the lodge site.

Brief replies from Grandma appear between acts as several spring weeks pass.
They reflect the protagonist's progress and offer emotional perspective or
general beaver wisdom, but never prescribe solutions to local problems. The
player does not see the protagonist's outgoing letters, preserving room to
imagine what they wrote.

## Act I — Willowbend

The protagonist repairs the remains of Willowbend's old dam complex. Water
returns to the floodplain, the first pond forms, and the frog becomes the
protagonist's first neighbor and friend. The protagonist begins building a
lodge and sees the first proof that their work creates more than a private
home.

As the pond fills, Eddy, a native Bonneville cutthroat trout, reveals the first
tradeoff. The new water creates useful pond habitat, but the unfinished dam's
flow path makes movement difficult and concentrates silt. Reed observes the
effects above and below the structure, then preserves a porous overflow or side
route Eddy can use when flow permits. Eddy later moves visibly among several
habitats and is joined by a small school. Reed learns that a helpful dam still
has to respond to the species and stream around it.

During a small celebration, a pulse of muddy meltwater carries charred branches
downstream. The dam holds, but the water shows that Willowbend cannot be secured
in isolation. A duck arrives from Aspen Meadow, reports that water there is
behaving strangely, and asks the protagonist to come upstream.

Before the protagonist leaves, a raccoon takes a small bundle of construction
material and flees upstream. The frog sees a thief; the player sees too little
to know whether that judgment is fair. The loss creates a brief inconvenience,
not renewed grinding or major lost progress.

## Act II — Aspen Meadow

The duck correctly understands that Aspen Meadow needs water but arrives with
an incomplete solution: build a large dam like the one at Willowbend. When the
protagonist reaches the meadow, they learn that simply raising the water would
solve one habitat problem while creating another. The protagonist must stop
copying their first success, observe the needs of several residents, and learn
to distribute water through a new adjustable construction.

The player configures that construction in one of two healthy but materially
different ways. A deep-pool configuration creates open water and a nesting
island favored by the duck. A braided-meadow configuration spreads shallow
channels through sedges and spring flowers favored by the butterfly. The
rabbit receives a safe high-ground warren and crossings before either choice,
so neither configuration destroys its home. The unpreferred resident stays and
adapts rather than guilting the player.

The configuration changes traversal, resident routines, resource availability,
optional activities, the region's spring-surge tasks, and its epilogue image.
The player can rebuild it later for a modest material cost, while dialogue
remembers the first configuration they completed.

The raccoon scavenges again during the Aspen Meadow work. Tracks and a few
carefully selected missing materials suggest that it is building something
rather than hoarding or destroying supplies. One resident remains angry while
another wonders where the raccoon sleeps. The trail and increasingly charred
runoff debris point toward Moraine Basin.

## Act III — Moraine Basin

The protagonist follows the raccoon's trail into the old burn scar and finds a
temporary shelter repeatedly damaged by runoff. The raccoon has been displaced
since the wildfire and scavenges because safe material and stable ground are
scarce. Feeding it a berry becomes an opening for communication rather than a
way to shoo away a pest.

The raccoon is sympathetic but not blameless: it knowingly took useful
community material and pulled loose wood from a vulnerable structure, worsening
a leak. It acted from fear and immediate need rather than malice. Once the
protagonist understands its situation, the raccoon acknowledges the harm and
helps repair more than it took. Responsibility and repair—not innocence—begin
to rebuild trust.

The raccoon chooses to remain in Moraine Basin. The protagonist helps establish
a safe den on rocky high ground above the flood path but near the recovering
riparian corridor. The raccoon contributes firsthand knowledge of post-fire
runoff routes and reaches useful deadfall on steep ground, becoming a genuine
engineering partner rather than a recipient of rescue.

Together they reject the idea of one enormous barrier against the snowmelt.
They prepare a connected system that gives water many safe places to go: small
porous debris dams slow flow and catch burned sediment, reopened side channels
spread runoff into floodplain pockets, and vulnerable banks and the high-ground
den are reinforced. The plan combines lessons from Willowbend and Aspen Meadow
instead of introducing a magical final machine.

As warm rain approaches, the protagonist briefly revisits the downstream
regions to prepare each one, including the current Aspen Meadow configuration.
The act ends when the connected watershed and its residents are ready for peak
runoff.

## Climax — The spring surge

A warm spring storm accelerates snowmelt in Moraine Basin, beginning a 10–15
minute active but forgiving sequence across the three regions. The surge
advances in authored stages after key tasks rather than on a hard failure timer,
so pausing to understand a prompt never causes disaster.

Each region presents one problem shaped by earlier construction. The current
Aspen Meadow configuration changes whether the protagonist manages one intense
spillway problem or several distributed repairs and rescues. Residents visibly
handle other needs, making the protagonist important without asking them to
save everyone alone. Strong preparation produces calmer routes and richer
payoff details; incomplete preparation creates extra repairs, muddier habitat,
and different dialogue but never destroys a major build or prevents the ending.

The final task returns to Willowbend. The protagonist protects the home that
began as a private goal by relying on the upstream system and the community.
Completing that task transitions into a large payoff sequence that follows the
water through the connected watershed and shows the restored regions holding
together.

## Ending — A living watershed

After the surge, every resident gathers at Willowbend for a modest celebration
around the completed lodge. The camera revisits each region: Aspen Meadow
retains the player's current configuration, the raccoon works near the
high-ground den, and every view contains signs of continuing recovery rather
than a magically healed wilderness.

After the gathering, the protagonist receives one final reply from Grandma.
She recognizes that they found more than a place to build without stating the
story's theme as a moral. At dawn, the protagonist quietly adds a small piece
to the dam or lodge simply because it needs care, then watches the living pond
wake up.

Credits follow. The completed save returns to calm free play, where the player
can revisit every region, reconfigure Aspen Meadow, finish optional requests,
maintain structures, speak with residents, and spend more time in the world.
